# Jung and Synchronicity

## Week 5 · SLOP3855

Is synchronicity a real category of event, or a name for a coincidence we
refuse to let go of?

```notes
Open with the question on the board, not the answer. Don't resolve it yet —
the whole deck is building toward holding both readings at once.
```

---

## A word that didn't exist before Jung

In 1952, the psychiatrist Carl Jung published an essay called *"Synchronicity:
An Acausal Connecting Principle."* He proposed a category of event that he
believed existing psychology had no vocabulary for.

**Synchronicity**: two or more events, connected not by cause and effect, but
by *meaning*, to the person experiencing them.

---

## The scarab

Jung's most frequently cited case, from his own clinical practice:

A patient is describing a dream about a golden scarab beetle. At that exact
moment, something taps at the consulting-room window. Jung opens it — a
real, scarab-like beetle flies in.

He treated the coincidence itself as clinically significant, not as an
interruption to the session.

```notes
This is Jung's own account, widely retold — flag that we only have his side
of it, and that "a beetle flew in during a session about a beetle" is exactly
the kind of story that gets told *because* it's striking, not a random sample
of everything that happens in therapy.
```

---

## Why not just call it a coincidence?

Jung's own background matters here. He had already built a theory of
**archetypes** — recurring symbolic patterns he believed were shared across
human minds, part of what he called the **collective unconscious**.

A coincidence that lines up with an archetype in progress, for Jung, wasn't
"just" chance. It was the same underlying structure surfacing in two places
at once — the dream, and the world.

---

## Why not just call it causation, either?

Jung was careful not to claim the beetle was *caused* by the dream, or the
dream by the beetle. That would be an ordinary (if extraordinary) causal
claim, testable in principle.

Synchronicity was pitched as a third option: connected by meaning, without
either causing the other. This is the most contested part of the whole
theory — a connection that isn't causal is doing a lot of unexamined work.

## Three objections

Base rates. Confirmation bias. Unfalsifiability.

```notes
Pause here — ask the room to guess what the three objections might be before
advancing. Most groups get at least two unprompted.
```

---

## Objection 1 — base rates

From week 2: across a life full of half-remembered dreams and half-remembered
insects, some dream is going to line up with some real event, purely by
chance, given enough attempts.

You don't remember the thousands of dreams that predicted nothing. You
remember the scarab.

---

## Objection 2 — confirmation bias

From week 1: once you're primed to notice "meaningful" coincidences, you get
better at noticing them and worse at noticing the misses.

A patient already deep in symbolic, dream-attentive work is exactly the
person most likely to register — and report — a coincidence like this one.

---

## Objection 3 — unfalsifiability

The hardest objection. If a matching event counts as synchronicity, and an
unmatching event just means the synchronicity hasn't happened *yet*, no
observation could ever count against the theory.

A claim that no possible evidence could disprove is doing something other
than describing the world.

---

## What survives

Not the metaphysical claim — that meaning-without-causation is a real
connective force in the universe. Serious philosophy of science has not been
kind to that part.

What survives is a **psychological** description: some experiences carry a
felt sense of significance that plain "it's a coincidence" doesn't capture —
and that felt sense is real, measurable, and worth studying, whatever
produces it.

---

## Forward to week 10

Jung didn't have the vocabulary for confirmation bias or illusory
correlation — those terms came later, from psychology, not psychiatry.

Week 10 names the mechanism properly. Keep the scarab in mind when you get
there.
