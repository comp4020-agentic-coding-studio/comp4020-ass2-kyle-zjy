This course studies things that get taken more seriously than the evidence for
them warrants — omens, astrology, tarot, numerology, the sense that a
recommendation algorithm "gets" you. Teaching that material carries an obvious
risk: build the wrong website around it, and the site itself becomes another
thing that gets taken more seriously than the evidence warrants. This page
says plainly what the course does to avoid that, so the choice is visible
rather than assumed.

## What the site will not do

No page on this site claims that a horoscope, a tarot spread, an I Ching
casting or a numerology reading has a confirmed predictive mechanism, because
none of them do. Where a lecture covers a divination system, it says what the
system actually does — draws a symbol, folds a coin, reduces a birthdate to a
digit — and separates that from what it is often taken to do. [Week
6](/lectures/week-06/) says this most directly: controlled studies asking
astrologers to match horoscopes to strangers using birth data alone have not
found accuracy above chance, and the lecture does not soften that finding to
make the topic more entertaining.

The site also avoids the interaction patterns that make an *actual* horoscope
site sticky — no daily push notification, no streak counter, no invented
urgency, no result gated behind sharing it first. Every activity on this site
can be closed and forgotten with nothing lost.

## Every activity discloses its own mechanism

The three interactive components used across the [lecture pages](/lectures/)
follow one rule: never simulate a power the course doesn't have.

- **Perception checks** ask you to notice something about your own perception
  (a shape, a pattern, a coincidence) and then show everyone the same
  explanation, regardless of what you picked. There is no correct answer to
  score.
- **Interpretation choices** show you several genuinely different readings of
  one ambiguous case and let you open any or all of them, in any order —
  because the lecture's point is usually that more than one reading survives
  contact with the evidence.
- **The oracle generator**, used where a lecture covers a working divination
  method, always ships with a visible "How this works" disclosure next to the
  reading itself, naming the exact mechanism (a random pick from a fixed list,
  most often) in the same place you read the output. [Build an
  Oracle](/assessments/build-an-oracle/) asks you to hold your own project to
  the same rule.

## The case-file numbering

Lecture pages carry a small stamp reading "CASE FILE 855-<var>NN</var>" next
to the date. It borrows the visual language of an archive — a place where
things are filed, dated and open to being reopened — because that is closer to
how this course wants a coincidence treated than either "sacred" or "silly."
The number is nothing more than the course's own numeric suffix and the week;
there is no hidden pattern in it to go looking for, which is itself a small,
deliberate joke about the whole subject of the course.

## Why the visual design looks the way it does

The site is typographic and diagrammatic rather than photographic or
decorative, on purpose. A course arguing that meaning gets projected onto
plain material shouldn't dress its own pages in mystical typography, starfields
or hand-drawn symbols — that would be the same trick the course spends twelve
weeks examining, aimed at its own students. What styling exists (the
[archive stamp](#the-case-file-numbering), the muted card grid, the restrained
palette) is meant to read as a research site, not a divination one.

## Where the course still leaves room for the feeling

None of this is an argument that the *feeling* of a meaningful coincidence is
fake or unworthy of attention — see [the homepage](/) and
[Week 12](/lectures/week-12/), which both turn this same scrutiny on the
website you are reading right now. The course's position, stated plainly, is
that the feeling is real and worth taking seriously as a feeling, which is a
different claim from the pattern itself carrying a hidden cause. Holding both
of those at once, rather than collapsing into either "it's all meaningless" or
"it must mean something," is the actual skill the [final
project](/assessments/final-project/) asks you to demonstrate.
