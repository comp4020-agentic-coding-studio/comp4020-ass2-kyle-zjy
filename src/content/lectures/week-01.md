Two costly mistakes are available to an animal deciding whether a rustle in
the grass is a predator: treat noise as signal (waste energy, look foolish),
or treat signal as noise (get eaten). Evolution did not need pattern detection
to be *accurate*. It needed it to be cheap and to fail in the survivable
direction. That asymmetry is the starting point for the whole course: humans
inherited a detector tuned to over-fire, and it has never been recalibrated
for a world made of headlines, horoscopes and recommendation algorithms
instead of grass.

Psychologists have names for the two halves of this. **Pareidolia** is seeing
a specific, recognisable shape in ambiguous sensory input — a face in a
power socket, a word in static. **Apophenia** is the broader tendency to see
connection or meaning in unrelated events — noticing that the phone rang right
as you thought of the caller, and feeling that this *means* something. Neither
is a malfunction. Both are the ordinary output of a system doing exactly what
it evolved to do, at a rate tuned for a much noisier and more dangerous
environment than a lecture theatre.

This week is deliberately the least dramatic in the course. There is no omen,
no oracle, no algorithm yet — just the raw machinery, so that every stranger
case later in the semester can be traced back to it rather than treated as
its own mystery.

## Try it

The activity below is the same coin-sequence problem researchers have used for
decades to study this effect. Guess first, then read what was actually going
on — the point isn't to score a right answer, it's to notice which sequence
*felt* more random, and ask why.
