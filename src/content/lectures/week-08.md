The **I Ching** ("Book of Changes") is one of the oldest continuously used
divination systems in the world, built on a genuinely elegant piece of
engineering: a physical randomising procedure (traditionally, tossing three
coins six times, or manipulating yarrow stalks) that selects one of 64
hexagrams, each attached to a fixed body of interpretive text refined over
centuries of commentary. Unlike a horoscope or a tarot reading, the random
process here is mechanically real — there is no sleight of hand in how the
hexagram is chosen.

That makes the I Ching the cleanest case this course can offer for a genuinely
interesting question: if the randomising step is legitimate, does that make
the reading legitimate too? The activity below builds a deliberately
simplified version — one random draw from five lines, with the mechanism
shown rather than hidden — so you can test your own reaction to a system that
is *provably* fair, in a way tarot and astrology are not.

Two things are worth separating. First, the coin toss is real randomness:
no information about your specific situation enters the system at any point.
Second, the *reading* of the result is where all the interpretive weight is
actually carried — a centuries-refined hexagram text, or five short lines
written for a demo, still needs a human to map it onto their actual problem.
That mapping is a real cognitive act, often a useful one, and entirely
independent of whether the coin toss "knew" anything.

## Try it

Ask a real question, then cast. Read "How this works" before you decide what
you think of the answer you got.
