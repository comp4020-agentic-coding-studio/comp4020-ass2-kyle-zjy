Carl Jung coined **synchronicity** to describe a category of event he thought
existing psychology had no room for: two things happening together, connected
not by cause and effect but by shared *meaning* to the person experiencing
them. His own frequently cited case involved a patient describing a dream
about a golden scarab beetle, at the exact moment a real scarab-like beetle
tapped on the consulting-room window. Jung treated the coincidence itself as
clinically significant — not a distraction from the therapy, but part of it.

This week's slide deck works through the concept properly: where it sits in
Jung's broader theory of archetypes and the collective unconscious, why he
needed a new term at all rather than reusing "coincidence," and the strongest
objections — chiefly, that "meaningfully connected without being causally
connected" is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work, and that every
example offered in its favour is also fully explicable by base rates and
selective memory, as in the activity below.

The aim is not to debunk Jung in one lecture — plenty of serious people have
found the concept useful as a description of a *psychological* event, whatever
its status as a claim about the world. The aim is to hold both readings at
once: something real is happening when a coincidence feels significant, and
that "something" may be entirely explicable without the coincidence itself
carrying any information at all.
