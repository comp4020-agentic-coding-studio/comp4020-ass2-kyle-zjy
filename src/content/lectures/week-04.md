Every culture that keeps written records has a theory of dreams, and almost
none of them agree with each other — which is itself a clue. If dreams
straightforwardly *meant* something, in the way a thermometer straightforwardly
reports a temperature, we would expect more convergence after several thousand
years of paying close attention to them. Instead we get dream dictionaries,
psychoanalytic frameworks, neuroscientific models and folk traditions that
each produce internally consistent readings of the same raw material.

That divergence is the week's real subject. A dream is unusually *interpretable*
compared to most experience: it is fragmentary, symbolic, largely forgotten
within minutes, and reconstructed from memory the moment you start describing
it to someone else — which means the "text" you're interpreting was already
edited by the same meaning-making machinery studied in week 1, before any
formal interpretive framework gets applied to it at all.

This week sits deliberately between weeks 3 and 5: an omen is a public,
external sign; a dream is a private, internal one; synchronicity next week
tries to argue the two aren't as different as they look. Notice, across all
three, how much of the "reading" happens after the event, in the retelling.
