Last week was about *why* the detector fires. This week is about a second,
compounding problem: even when someone tries to reason carefully about chance,
the reasoning itself tends to be wrong, in specific and repeatable ways.

The birthday paradox in the activity below is one example. Another is the
**gambler's fallacy** — the belief that a coin "due" for tails after five
heads is more likely to land tails, when in fact each flip is independent and
the coin has no memory. A third is the sheer size of the "reference class" a
coincidence is drawn from. If you notice one striking coincidence a month,
across a 20,000-day life that is over 650 opportunities for something
mundane to line up in a way that feels impossible — not because your life is
unusually fated, but because the population of "days in which something could
coincide" is enormous, and only the hits get remembered.

This is not an argument that probability *explains away* every coincidence, or
that every striking event is secretly mundane once you do the arithmetic. It
is an argument that "how surprising does this feel" is a bad instrument for
measuring "how surprising is this," and the gap between the two is wide,
consistent, and well studied. Kahneman and Tversky's research on heuristics
and biases — much of which the course returns to in week 10 — is the
intellectual backbone of this claim.
