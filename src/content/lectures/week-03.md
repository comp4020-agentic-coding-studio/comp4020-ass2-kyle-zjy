Omens are one of the oldest recorded technologies for managing uncertainty.
Roman augurs read the flight of birds before a military campaign. Mesopotamian
priests read the livers of sacrificed animals. A modern reader who would never
consult an augur still touches wood, avoids walking under ladders, or feels a
flicker of unease about the number of a hotel room. The specific vocabulary
changes; the underlying move does not.

That move has three parts, and this week's activity is designed to make you
try all three rather than pick the "right" one, because all three are
operating at once. First, an event has to be *noticed* — singled out from the
thousands of neutral events in a day. Second, it has to be *linked* to an
outcome, usually one that already worried you. Third, the link has to survive
contact with what actually happens: a hit gets told as a story for years, a
miss is quietly forgotten within the hour. None of this requires the omen to
have any causal connection to the outcome at all — the entire system runs on
noticing, linking and selective memory.

This doesn't make omens meaningless as *practices*. A pre-exam ritual can
genuinely reduce anxiety and improve performance, whatever its official
justification. The question this course keeps returning to is not "does this
work" but "what is it actually doing, and is that the same thing it claims to
be doing."

## Try it

Pick each of the three readings in turn — they aren't ranked, and the point is
to notice that none of them is simply wrong.
